How does a shrimp fire a bubble?

Change the jet, the surrounding water pressure, and the target distance. Then fire a slowed-down, simplified snap to see the difference between the water jet, the vapour cavity, and the shock wave.

Change the conditions

Faster flow can lower local pressure and make cavitation more likely. Around 25 m/s was reported in one laboratory study.

Greater ambient pressure makes forming a vapour cavity harder. This slider is a simplified stand-in for pressure changes with depth.

Moves the target. Pressure pulses spread outward and weaken with distance; this does not calculate injury or the prey's chances.

With this jet and pressure setting, the model shows a vortex cavity that collapses and emits a pressure pulse.

Important: This is a teaching animation, not a measured shrimp shot, calibrated flow solver, temperature estimate or prey-damage prediction. The cavitation threshold, visual sizes, timing and pressure-wave brightness are deliberately illustrative. In real water, claw shape, vortex geometry, dissolved gas and ambient conditions matter. The flash shown here is exaggerated: real shrimpoluminescence is extremely faint and fleeting.
